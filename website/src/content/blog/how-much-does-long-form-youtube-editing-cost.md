"How much does editing cost?" is the first question almost every creator asks, and the honest answer is: it depends on what you're asking an editor to do. Two quotes for "a 15-minute YouTube video" can be wildly different because they describe different jobs.

This guide explains what actually drives the cost of long-form editing, the common pricing models, and how to compare quotes so you're paying for what you need.

## What drives the price

### 1. Raw footage, not just final length

Editors spend a large part of their time watching, organising and selecting footage. A 15-minute final video cut from 20 minutes of tightly scripted recording is a very different job from a 15-minute video cut from three hours of unscripted vlog footage.

When you request a quote, always state **both** your typical final length and your typical raw footage length.

### 2. Format and complexity

Roughly from simplest to most involved:

- **Podcasts and interviews** — mostly about clean audio, camera switching and removing dead air. Long, but structurally straightforward.
- **Talking-head videos** — tight cuts, punch-ins, text, B-roll and music.
- **Educational and business videos** — the above plus diagrams, charts and callouts that need to be accurate.
- **Documentary and storytelling** — heavy on structure, music, sourced B-roll and pacing decisions.
- **Faceless videos** — every second needs a visual, so B-roll and motion graphics carry the whole video.

The more the editor has to *create* rather than *cut*, the more the edit costs.

### 3. Motion graphics and custom visuals

Animated titles, lower thirds, data visualisations and custom graphics are where costs climb fastest. A video with a handful of text callouts and a video with animated charts in every section are priced very differently.

### 4. Turnaround

Faster turnaround requires scheduling capacity. Services with a standard turnaround window can plan around it; rush jobs on short notice usually cost more or simply aren't available.

### 5. Revisions

Some editors include a fixed number of revision rounds and charge for more. Others include unlimited revisions. Check which — a cheaper quote with paid revisions can end up costing more.

### 6. What's bundled

Thumbnail design, music licensing, stock footage and sound effects may or may not be included. Make sure you're comparing like with like.

### 7. Volume

Monthly plans are often cheaper per video than one-off projects, because predictable volume lets an editor plan their schedule and learn your style once.

## Common pricing models

| Model | How it works | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Per video | A fixed price per finished video within agreed limits | Limits on length or footage — read them |
| Per finished minute | Price scales with the final runtime | Doesn't reflect how much footage you send |
| Hourly | You pay for time spent | Hard to budget; slow editors cost more |
| Monthly package | A set number of videos per month | What happens to unused videos, and how scope is defined |

Per-video and monthly pricing are the easiest to budget for, because you know the cost before you send footage.

## How to compare quotes fairly

Send every editor the same information:

1. Format and a link to your channel
2. Typical final length **and** raw footage length
3. Videos per month
4. Two or three reference videos showing the style you want
5. Your required turnaround
6. Whether you need a thumbnail

Then compare on a per-video basis, including revisions and anything bundled. Ask each one for a paid test on a real video before committing to volume — our guide on [how to hire a YouTube video editor](/blog/how-to-hire-a-youtube-video-editor/) covers how to run one.

## Cheap vs. expensive: what you're really paying for

A very low price usually means less time spent per video, which shows up in pacing, audio and attention to detail. A very high price may include a level of custom animation you don't need.

The right price is the one that matches the polish your format needs, delivered reliably enough that you can publish on schedule. Reliability is easy to undervalue: an editor who is slightly cheaper but misses deadlines costs you uploads.

## Ways to reduce your editing cost

- **Record tighter.** A rough outline before you hit record means less footage to sort through.
- **Mark mistakes as you record.** A clap or a spoken "cut" makes retakes easy to find.
- **Send organised files.** Clearly named folders save editing time.
- **Write a reusable brief.** Style notes you send once save time on every video.
- **Commit to volume** if you publish regularly — monthly plans usually lower the per-video price.

## The bottom line

Editing cost is driven by raw footage, complexity, graphics, turnaround, revisions and volume. Get quotes on the same brief, compare per video, and test before committing.
