Hiring an editor is one of the highest-leverage decisions a YouTube creator makes. Done well, it gives you back the hours you currently spend in a timeline and lets you publish on a schedule you can actually keep. Done badly, it costs you weeks of back-and-forth and a pile of videos you have to re-edit yourself.

This guide walks through the process step by step: deciding what you need, where to look, how to evaluate an editor properly, and what to agree before the first video.

## Start by defining what you actually need

Most hiring mistakes happen before anyone is contacted. "I need an editor" is too vague to hire against. Write down answers to these questions first:

- **What format are your videos?** Talking-head, podcast, documentary-style, tutorial, faceless? Each needs different instincts.
- **How long are they?** A 12-minute talking-head video and a 90-minute podcast are completely different jobs.
- **How much raw footage do you record per video?** Editors price and plan around raw footage as much as final length.
- **How many videos per month?** One video is a project. Four a month is a working relationship.
- **What level of polish do you want?** Clean cuts and music, or heavy motion graphics, sound design and custom B-roll?
- **What turnaround do you need?** Work backwards from your publishing schedule.

These answers become your brief. They also stop you comparing editors on price alone when they are quoting for different work.

## Decide between a freelancer and an editing service

There are broadly two ways to get your videos edited.

**A freelance editor** is one person. The upside is a direct relationship and potentially a very particular style. The downside is that your publishing schedule depends on one person's availability, health and other clients. If they disappear for two weeks, so does your upload.

**An editing service or agency** is a team with a process. You typically get more predictable turnaround, set pricing and continuity if one editor is unavailable. The trade-off is that you are buying into their workflow, so it matters that the workflow is good.

Neither is automatically better. If you publish weekly and your channel is a business, reliability usually matters more than anything else. If you publish occasionally and want a very specific artistic voice, a freelancer may suit you.

## Where to look

- **Your own audience and network.** Creators in your niche often know editors who already understand the format.
- **Credits on videos you admire.** Many editors are credited in descriptions. If you like how a video feels, find out who cut it.
- **Freelance marketplaces.** Large supply, wide range of quality. Expect to filter hard.
- **Specialist editing services.** Useful when you want consistency and published pricing rather than negotiating per video.

Whichever route you take, judge by the work, not the profile.

## How to evaluate an editor's portfolio

A portfolio tells you more than any interview — if you know what to look for.

1. **Look for your format.** A brilliant short-form editor is not automatically good at keeping a 20-minute video moving. Long-form editing is about structure and pacing across many minutes, not just energy in the first ten seconds.
2. **Watch whole videos, not just showreels.** A reel shows the best three seconds of everything. The middle of a long video is where weak editing shows.
3. **Check the audio.** Clean, consistent dialogue and well-mixed music are a strong signal of care. Bad audio loses viewers faster than plain visuals.
4. **Notice restraint.** Good editors know when *not* to add an effect. Constant zooms, whooshes and captions on every word can make a video tiring to watch.
5. **Ask what they were given.** A great edit from great footage is less informative than a great edit from ordinary footage.

## Run a paid test — always

The single most reliable way to hire is a paid test edit on your own footage. A portfolio shows what an editor did for someone else; a test shows what they will do for you.

Keep the test realistic:

- Use a real video you intend to publish, not a toy clip.
- Give the same brief you would give long-term — format, references, length, deadline.
- Pay for it. Unpaid tests attract the wrong people and give the right ones a reason to say no.
- Include one round of feedback. How someone handles revisions matters as much as the first draft.

## Questions to ask before you hire

- What does your process look like from receiving footage to final delivery?
- What is your typical turnaround for a first draft?
- How are revisions handled, and how many are included?
- How do you want footage and assets delivered?
- Who will actually edit my videos, and what happens if they are unavailable?
- What do you need from me in a brief?
- Is the thumbnail included, or separate?
- How is pricing structured — per video, per minute of final runtime, or per hour?

Clear, specific answers are a good sign. Vague answers about "it depends" on every question usually mean there is no real process.

## Red flags

- **No portfolio in your format**, or only short-form clips.
- **Guaranteed views, subscribers or virality.** No editor can promise how an algorithm or an audience will respond. Editing improves the video; it doesn't control the outcome.
- **Pricing that changes after the footage arrives** without a clear reason agreed in advance.
- **No defined revision process.** "We'll fix whatever you need" sounds generous until revisions take a week each.
- **Slow, vague communication during the hiring stage.** It rarely improves after you've paid.

## Set the relationship up to succeed

Once you've chosen an editor, the first few videos shape everything that follows.

- **Write a short style guide.** Fonts, colours, intro/outro, caption style, music preferences, things you never want to see.
- **Share references.** Two or three videos whose editing you like do more than paragraphs of description.
- **Give specific feedback.** "Cut the tangent at 04:12" is actionable. "Make it pop more" isn't.
- **Keep a consistent workflow.** Same folder structure, same naming, same brief template. Consistency on your side creates consistency on theirs.
